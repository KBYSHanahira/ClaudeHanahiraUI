# Writing

A look-up for when the deliverable is text for someone to read: an email, message, document, report, post, summary. The user asked for the thing, so deliver the finished thing — not tips, not an outline, not three variants unless asked.

## Before writing

Know three things; ask only if one is missing and cannot be inferred:

1. **Reader** — who, what they already know, and how much time they will give it.
2. **Purpose** — what the reader should know or do after reading. If it is an action, it must be findable in five seconds.
3. **Constraints** — language, length, channel, house style, things that must or must not be said.

Then be able to state the point in two sentences. If that is not possible yet, the problem is the thinking, not the wording; sort that out first.

## Writing it

- **Point first.** The conclusion, the request or the decision opens the piece; background follows for those who want it.
- **One idea per paragraph**, and its first sentence says what the paragraph is about.
- **Concrete over abstract.** The number, the name, the date, the example. "Delivery moves from 3 March to 10 March", not "there is a slight delay".
- **Plain words, real sentences.** Short where possible, but never clipped into fragments the reader has to rebuild. Define a term the reader may not know, once.
- **One name per thing.** If it is "the invoice" in paragraph one, it is not "the bill" in paragraph three.
- **State things positively and directly.** Say what is true; drop the sentence that only sets up the next one.
- **No filler**: no "I hope this finds you well", "it's worth noting", "in today's fast-paced world", no closing line that repeats the body.
- **Formatting follows the content.** Steps are numbered, comparisons are tables, everything else is paragraphs. Email and chat usually carry no headers at all.

## By kind

| Kind | What makes it work |
|---|---|
| Email | A subject line that states the content. The ask or the news in the first two lines. One email, one purpose. A clear next step with who and when |
| Bad news | The news first, plainly; the reason in a sentence; what happens next and what the reader can do. One apology, no grovelling, no blame |
| Chat message | Complete on its own: the reader should not need the thread. Lead with what is needed from them |
| Report or document | A summary at the top that a busy reader could stop after. Sections that answer the reader's questions in the order they would ask them. Sources for facts |
| Summary | Only what the source says: decisions, action items with owner and date, open questions. No opinions added, no gaps filled in |
| Post or announcement | The one thing the audience should remember, first. Written for the people reading, not for the people launching |
| Instructions | One action per step, in order, each with what the reader should see when it worked |

## Facts and placeholders

Never invent a fact, name, figure, date or quotation. What is needed and not known becomes a visible placeholder in the reader's language — `[new delivery date]`, `[วันที่ส่งมอบใหม่]` — and the reply lists the placeholders in one line so none ships by accident. Current facts are looked up and cited, not recalled.

## Language and register

Write in the language the reader reads, which may differ from the chat's. Match the register the relationship calls for: Thai and Japanese business writing carry politeness that English leaves out, and the reverse. When translating, translate the meaning and the tone, not the word order; keep names, product terms and figures exact.

## Editing someone's text

Change what was asked. "Fix the grammar" is not "rewrite it". Keep the author's voice and structure, show what changed if the text is long, and mention — once, briefly — a real problem outside the brief, such as a wrong date or a missing ask.

## Before delivering

Read it as the reader: Is the point in the first lines? Can they act? Is every number and name right? Would any sentence be missed if cut? A long or high-stakes piece — more than a page, or going to a client, a manager or the public — goes to a fresh critic with the brief before it goes to the user.
